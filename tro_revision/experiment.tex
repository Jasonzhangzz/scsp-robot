\section{Experiment I: Contact model Evaluation}
\label{sec:experiment}
\label{sec:scm_evaluation}

To validate the fidelity of the contact models, we compare MuJoCo's native quadratic-programming (QP) model, ComFree, SCM, and ContactSDF against a reference combining MJWarp collision detection and integration with fully coupled friction-pyramid LCP contact dynamics. The reference uses two $h=0.001\,\mathrm{s}$ substeps, while the predictors use one $h=0.002\,\mathrm{s}$ step. We report the linear- and angular-velocity errors of the post-step object velocity $\boldsymbol{v}^{+}$ relative to LCP under four contact states: contact transition, sliding, sticking, and the reference-path rollout. The benchmark uses seven objects, 32 initial states per object and contact state, and 10 rollout windows per rollout state. The results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:scm_velocity_error}.

For SCM, we smooth the initial impulse using $\operatorname{smax}_{T}(x,0)=T\log(1+\exp(x/T))$, with $T=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N\,s}$. It retains the same free velocity and global amplitude correction, including the clipping of $\alpha$ to $[0,1]$, and introduces no spring-damper response time. Initial poses are obtained by native MuJoCo settling; the reported errors therefore include recovery from residual penetration in those poses.

We further apply the optimal impulse at the selected contact to control the object and evaluate whether each contact model can be used for CSO. The results are shown in Table~\ref{tab:cso_contact_guidance}. Since this experiment focuses on contact-guidance feasibility, we report only success rates under synchronous inference without observation latency. All methods achieve high success rates. Although models such as MuJoCo may require fewer execution steps because of their more accurate contact modeling, SCM has a shorter inference time, which is important for obtaining timely and accurate contact information. Overall, SCM is a feasible contact model for CSO.

\section{Experiment II: Nonprehensile Manipulation}
\label{sec:experiment_ii}

This section evaluates the SCSP framework through nonprehensile manipulation experiments, which represent a class of contact-rich manipulation tasks characterized by frequent contact-state transitions and unstable control. The tasks consist of on-ground rotation and on-ground flipping. On-ground rotation requires the robot to move an object to a target pose on the ground through 2D pushing and rotating, whereas on-ground flipping involves 6D manipulation. We select a set of irregularly shaped objects from the ContactDB dataset \cite{brahmbhatt2019contactdb} for validation. The objects are initialized at random poses with 
$x_{\text{init}}, y_{\text{init}} \in [-0.3, 0.3]$ m, 
and Euler angles $(\phi_{\text{init}}, \theta_{\text{init}} , \psi_{\text{init}})$ 
uniformly sampled from $[-\pi, \pi]$ rad. We define the conditions of task success as: 
\begin{equation}
    \left\| \boldsymbol{p}_o - \boldsymbol{p}_{\text{goal}} \right\|
\le 0.02 \,\text{[m]}, \ 
1 - (\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\text{goal}}^\top \boldsymbol{\psi}_o)^2
\le \textcolor{red}{0.015}, \label{eq:pose_success}
\end{equation}
\textcolor{red}{where the orientation tolerance corresponding to the quaternion error threshold is $14.1^\circ$.} A trial is considered successful if the conditions are satisfied within 2500 rollout steps.

Simulations are conducted in \texttt{MuJoCo} \cite{todorov2012mujoco} and  \texttt{IsaacGym} \cite{makoviychuk2021isaac} with a fixed integration time step $h=0.02 \text{s}$. For the sampling method in our CSO module, we use \texttt{CoACD} \cite{wei2022approximate} for convex decomposition of the objects and \texttt{trimesh} for mesh processing and local frame construction. CSO is solved using batched Levenberg--Marquardt (LM), while CPO is solved using \texttt{acados} SQP-RTI with HPIPM and a solver tolerance of $10^{-4}$. Unless otherwise stated, experiments are conducted on a desktop computer with an Intel i7-13620H processor, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU.

We applied certain randomization to the environment and object parameters. The friction coefficients of the object $\mu_{\text{o}}$, fingertip $\mu_{\text{r}}$, and ground $\mu_{\text{env}}$ are all set to 0.5 with random perturbations of $\pm 0.2$. The object mass is set to $0.1$ kg and randomly sampled from the discrete set
$\{0.01,0.1,1.0,10.0\}$ kg. We set $\boldsymbol{M}_o=\text{diag}(50,50,50,0.05,0.05,0.05)$ and $\boldsymbol{K}_r=300 \boldsymbol{I}_7$, and the dynamic parameters used in SCM are not measured precisely, but are roughly set to their mean values.

For CSO, the default configuration samples $n_s=70$ candidate contacts, retains the two lowest-cost candidates, and uses a one-step prediction with 20 batched LM iterations. CSO and CPO share a prediction step of $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$ and pose weights $w_{\mathrm{pos}}=500$ and $w_{\mathrm{quat}}=20$. CSO uses force and tangential-force regularization weights of $0.01$ and $0.03$, respectively, with a $3\,\mathrm{N}$ force-command limit corresponding to $\hat{\lambda}^{n}_{\max}\approx1.59\,\mathrm{N}$ under the configured friction pyramid. CPO uses $N=5$, componentwise control bounds $|u_j|\leq0.005\,\mathrm{m}$, and weights $w_{\mathrm{lp}}=1$, $w_u=50$, and $w_{\mathrm{cso}}=10$. Its lifting and reference-contact attraction weights are both 20, while object-center attraction and repulsive-field terms are disabled. During the flipping stage, the orientation error measures alignment of the object's target upright axis with the world vertical, leaving yaw unconstrained. Let $\boldsymbol{a}_g=\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\mathrm{goal}})^\top e_z$, where $e_z=[0,0,1]^\top$. The normalized upright error is $\ell_{\mathrm{stab}}(\boldsymbol{x}_o) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1-e_z^\top\boldsymbol{R}(\boldsymbol{x}_o)\boldsymbol{a}_g\right]$. The flipping-stage object cost is $\ell_{\mathrm{cso}}=500\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}+20\ell_{\mathrm{stab}}$, with the full quaternion-error weight set to zero. Here $\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}=\|\boldsymbol{p}_o-\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{goal}}\|^2$, except for the Stanford bunny, which uses $\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}=\max(\|\boldsymbol{p}_{o,xy}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{goal},xy}\|-0.08\,\mathrm{m},0)^2$ to allow vertical motion and planar displacement within an $8\,\mathrm{cm}$ radius during flipping.

From Table \ref{tab:comparison}, \rev{we select methods that are applicable to 6D manipulation and can manipulate objects with arbitrary geometries, rather than being restricted to regular shapes,} as baselines, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \rev{\textbf{DyWA}~\cite{lyu2025dywa}: An end-to-end 6D nonprehensile policy evaluated on unseen object geometries using the released single-view student policy in \texttt{IsaacGym}.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{ContactSDF}~\cite{yang2024contactsdf}: A differentiable multi-contact model based on signed distance functions (SDFs), which provides closed-form collision detection and time-stepping prediction for contact-rich manipulation.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{STOCS-3D}~\cite{zhang2025simultaneous}: An offline method that jointly optimizes contact selection and a contact-implicit trajectory optimization (CITO) problem for 3D manipulation. We use STOCS-3D to generate the CITO trajectory offline and execute the resulting trajectory with operational-space control (OSC).}
    \item \rev{\textbf{Sampling}~\cite{venkatesh2025approximating}: A hierarchical method that samples candidate contacts and evaluates them with a lower-level CIMPC controller. We replace its original C3 model with $f_{\text{cf}}$ for a common contact model.}
    \item \rev{\textbf{CF-MPC}~\cite{jin2024complementarity}: A complementarity-free MPC method with a closed-form contact model and limited explicit contact-selection capability.}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Functional Analysis of SCSP Components}
\label{sec:component_ablation}
\textbf{Ranking strategy (A1--A3):} CPO strictly follows CSO-optimal guidance $p_r^*$ (A1), always executes the nearest contact $p_{\text{near}}$ (A2), or follows simple heuristic guidance (A3).

\textbf{CPO (A6--A7):} A6 removes the phase-dependent attraction and repulsion potentials and directly optimizes motion trajectories; A7 directly explores contact trajectories using MPPI~[1] with the cost $\ell_{\mathrm{A7}} = \omega_{\text{o}}\ell_{\text{o}} + \omega_{\text{cso}}\ell_{\text{cso}}$,

\subsubsection{Counterfactual Analysis of the Ranking Strategy}
\label{sec:ranking_ablation}
A1--A3 evaluate alternative contact-selection policies at the task level. We further assess the ranking strategy at individual decision points using matched counterfactual rollouts.

Let $r_T=\mathbb{I}(\mathrm{success})$ be the terminal reward. For a state $s_t$ and ranking decision $b$, the discounted rollout value is
\begin{equation}
J(s_t,b)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_t,b)}
\left[\gamma^{T}\mathbb{I}(\mathrm{success})\right],
\qquad \gamma=0.99,
\end{equation}
where $T$ counts the actual execution steps from $s_t$ to trial termination, including all detour and contact-transition steps, rather than contact-selection events.

For each object and task, we reconstruct one successful synchronous trajectory using seeds drawn from existing successful-rollout records, and uniformly sample 5 distinct states with measured physical contact, excluding transit and detour motion, where the CSO guidance differs from the nearby contact. The simulator and controller histories are reconstructed by replaying the complete prefix, checking observations, commands, and ranking decisions up to the sampled state. We compare the unmodified RS rollout with a counterfactual rollout that makes the opposite decision at that state and protects the selected contact target from ranking overrides until physical contact with that target is established or the trial terminates. CPO continues closed-loop replanning during this event, without a fixed commitment horizon. Once the selected contact is established, the counterfactual rollout resumes the same RS policy as the original rollout. Task parameters remain unchanged. Let $J_{\mathrm{RS}}$ and $J_{\mathrm{cf}}$ denote the two rollout values at $s_t$. Their normalized advantage is
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{A}(s_t)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{J_{\mathrm{RS}}-J_{\mathrm{cf}}}
{\max\{J_{\mathrm{RS}},J_{\mathrm{cf}}\}},
& \max\{J_{\mathrm{RS}},J_{\mathrm{cf}}\}>0,\\[4pt]
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
Here $\widetilde{A}\in[-1,1]$, and $\widetilde{A}>0$ indicates that the ranking strategy is preferred. A value of $1$ ($-1$) means that only the RS (counterfactual) rollout succeeds. For two successful rollouts, this normalization depends only on the difference in their remaining completion steps, removing the common discount scale associated with long remaining trajectories.

In these synchronous rollouts, SCSP replans every $0.02\,\mathrm{s}$ of simulated time, corresponding to a system update frequency of $f_{\mathrm{SCSP}}=50\,\mathrm{Hz}$. We report the physical contact-switching frequency $f_{\mathrm{switch}}$ per simulated second, counting a switch only when the robot leaves the previous contact and establishes a distinct contact patch. The probabilities $P(p_{\text{near}})$ and $P(p_r^*)$ are the fractions of nearest-contact and CSO-optimal-contact selections among all eligible binary contact decisions in the original successful trajectories. The CSO-optimal contact-point switching frequency $f_r^*$ counts changes of $p_r^*$ per simulated second, using a 1\,cm displacement threshold in the object's frame to exclude rigid object motion and mesh jitter. Probabilities and frequencies use complete original trajectories, rather than the sampled counterfactual branches.

We normalize each sampled pair before aggregation and retain all 5 sampled states per trajectory, including $\widetilde{A}=0$, when calculating $P(\widetilde{A}>0)$ and the mean and sample standard deviation of $\widetilde{A}$ in Table~\ref{tab:ranking_advantage}.

\subsection{Real-world Experiments}

Finally, we validate the robustness of SCSP to in real-world experiments. Most model-based methods perform well in simulation, but in real-world experiments, they often rely on highly accurate perception systems, such as motion capture. The main challenge in replacing exact perception with purely vision-based perception is that errors in pose observation can perturb contact estimation, leading to incorrect inference of contact states and consequently inaccurate contact dynamics. To the best of our knowledge, our method is the first model-based approach that can achieve purely vision-based, complex geometries, model mismatch contact-rich manipulation.

\subsubsection{Experimental Setup}
We conduct experiments using a Franka Emika Panda equipped with a 3D-printed end-effector, and arbitrarily select 4 irregularly shaped objects: Duck, Bunny, Hand, and Xbox Controller, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:illustrative_example_1}. We evaluate on-ground rotation on Duck and Bunny, and on-ground flipping on Bunny, Hand, and Xbox Controller. The object CAD models are obtained from the ContactDB dataset and online repositories. To validate the robustness of our method under inaccurate dynamics, we provide only rough estimates of the object’s physical parameters such as mass and friction coefficients. Moreover, instead of relying on precise motion capture for exact perception, we perform state estimation by \texttt{FoundationPose} \cite{wen2024foundationpose} using a single-view camera (Realsense D435i). In real-world experiments, precise object–environment contact locations are not directly observable. To address this, we approximately reconstruct the task environment in \texttt{MuJoCo} for contact estimation, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:illustrative_example_i}. We perform a simple calibration procedure to determine the object’s scale parameter and positional offsets in the simulator to eliminate contact estimation misclassification. Specifically, we use Franka’s kinesthetic teaching mode to move the end-effector and make contact with several randomly selected locations on the object, and then tune the object’s scale and the offsets $[\Delta x, \Delta y, \Delta z]$ such that the simulator can approximately predict whether the end-effector is in contact with the object. The goal pose range, success conditions, and maximum execution steps for the task are identical to those in the simulation experiments. Sampling and DyWA use the same settings as in the previous section, and the outputs of DyWA are executed using an impedance controller.

The OSC controller is implemented in \textsf{c++}. Unlike in simulation experiments, control in real-world deployment is subject to disturbances, leading to control lag and steady-state errors. To address this, we designed an adaptive controller for precise control, which we refer to as the Incremental OSC. We introduce an intermediate position vector $\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{inc}} = \boldsymbol{p}_{\text{inc}} + \boldsymbol{K}_{\text{inc}}\boldsymbol{u}$,
and replace $\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{pos}}$  with $\boldsymbol{e}_{\text{pos}}=\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{inc}}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{ee}}$. The steady-state error is adaptively eliminated under the high-frequency self-integrating effect. In the experiment, we set
$\boldsymbol{K}_{\text{inc}}=0.01 \boldsymbol{I}_3$, $\boldsymbol{K}_x = \text{diag}(500,500,500,10,10,10)$ and $\boldsymbol{D}_x = \sqrt{2\boldsymbol{K}_x}$.

We first evaluate the success rate of SCSP across different objects and tabletop materials to assess its robustness under model inaccuracies. The experimental results are shown in Fig. \ref{fig:results_real_world_flip}, \ref{fig:real_world_continous}, and Table~\ref{tab:real_world_success}. They indicate that our method maintains strong performance under perception noise and model mismatch.

We conducted each experiment on two different tabletop materials: smooth and rough as in Fig. \ref{fig:real_world_disturbance}. For the on-ground rotation experiments, the smooth surface denotes a tabletop with low friction, whereas the rough surface denotes cotton clothes. For the on-ground flipping experiments, which require greater friction, we use foam padding as the rough surface and cotton cloth as the smooth surface. Note that our method does not measure friction coefficients, object mass, or other physical parameters, but instead uses coarse estimates. Additionally, biases in contact estimation and noise in object pose estimation can interfere with the decision-making of SCSP. The experiment results suggest that SCSP can effectively cope with model inaccuracies and disturbances, which is typically challenging for model-based methods. 

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:real_world_success}, SCSP achieved a 99.2\% success rate (119/120) in the on-ground rotation experiments with complex object geometries and also showed strong performance in the flipping tasks, whereas the comparison methods failed in nearly all flipping experiments. We observe that the failures of DyWA mainly arose from contact slippage and violations of the Franka acceleration limits during pressing, while the failures of Sampling were primarily caused by the selection of unstable contact locations. Due to the complex geometry and relatively smooth material of the 3D printed objects, the fingertip sometimes fails to establish stable contact with the object, resulting in relative sliding that causes the execution of the contact plan planned by SCSP to fail. Additionally, surface friction also affects the success rate of flipping. As shown in the results, SCSP achieves a notably higher success rate on the rough surface compared with the smooth surface.

We further conduct continuous manipulation experiments, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:real_world_continous}. During manipulation, we repeatedly perturb the object pose to evaluate the real-time performance, closed-loop planning capability, and robustness of SCSP under accumulated perception errors. Under repeated perturbations, both the pose estimated from \texttt{FoundationPose} and the contact estimation become biased. However, SCSP exhibits strong replanning capability and consistently completes the continuous manipulation tasks under disturbances.

We combine the preceding experimental results to analyze the source of SCSP’s robustness to model mismatch. We conclude that: 1) the ablation study reveals that optimality of the contact prior is not necessary. Although the prior is important for contact planning, even a coarse contact location selection as a prior can still bring substantial performance improvement. This observation also provides insight into the role of the contact-selection model: how to properly connect the prior with the planning framework seems to be more important than the optimality and accuracy of the prior itself. The designs of CSO and the ranking strategy are both developed with this perspective. 2) CPO has a strong closed-loop nature. The real-time performance and robustness of CIMPC methods have been thoroughly validated in previous work. CPO is a prior-guided contact planning framework built upon the CIMPC formulation. It inherits the robustness of CIMPC while improving manipulation diversity. Therefore, the SCSP framework is able to achieve robust performance.
