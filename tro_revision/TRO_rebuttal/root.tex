\reviewer{Reviewer 1}

\question{1}{The CSO-as-MIQP claim is under-specified}

Section III-D states that Eq. (32) is formulated as an MIQP, that the inner loop constitutes a QP, and that the overall CSO is an efficient MIQP. This is plausible only under additional assumptions that are not made explicit.

For a fixed sampled contact point, fixed contact geometry, fixed Jacobians, quadratic objective, polyhedral friction constraints, and an explicit active-set or binary-variable encoding of
\begin{equation}
 \hat{\lambda}_{\text{env},i}=\max(A_i\hat{\lambda}_r+b_i,0),
\end{equation}
the problem can be written as a region-wise QP or as an MIQP. However, the paper does not provide the MIQP variables, big-$M$ or indicator constraints, active-set constraints, or the precise quadratic form being solved. The implementation section also states that the CSO inner loop is solved using CasADi with SNOPT, which is an SQP/NLP solver rather than a QP or MIQP solver.

The paper should either present the exact QP/MIQP formulation and solver details, or weaken the claim to finite candidate search with local continuous force optimization under an SCM approximation.

\answer
Thank you for pointing out this inconsistency. We agree that our
previous characterization of CSO as an MIQP, and of its inner problem
as a QP, was imprecise.

In our actual implementation, the sampled contact location is not
encoded as an integer decision variable, and we do not introduce
binary variables, big-$M$ constraints, indicator constraints, or use
a mixed-integer solver. Instead, the discrete contact-location variable
is handled explicitly through the finite candidate set
$\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{avail}}$. For each candidate contact location,
we solve a continuous optimization problem over the robot contact
forces and then compare the resulting costs across candidates.

We need to correct an omission in our previous description. In addition to constructing a closed-form contact-dynamics approximation, SCM applies \ndel{one smooth residual correction on the coupled Delassus operator} \nadd{$K=3$ unrolled Gauss--Seidel sweeps over the environment contacts (Eq.~(27))} to the \badd{diagonal} environment impulse \badd{estimate}. \ndel{This correction introduces an additional coupled operation} \nadd{Each sweep evaluates each contact force from the object velocity already updated by the preceding contacts and sweeps and therefore introduces a sequential coupling}, so the resulting problem is even farther from a QP formulation. The previous implementation solved the continuous subproblem serially with SNOPT; the current implementation replaces this with GPU-parallel CEM. Overall, CSO is a lightweight optimization problem that permits efficient parallel solution, but it does not satisfy a QP formulation.

The revised formulation therefore does not introduce binary variables, big-$M$ constraints, indicator constraints, or a mixed-integer solve. \addrev{The previous ``MIQP cost time'' entry has been removed from the computation-time table (Table~IX); the table now reports the CSO solving time of the GPU-parallel CEM over all candidates, consistent with Table~V.}
\question{2}{The SCM assumptions and error bound are too weak}

The surrogate contact model relies on fixed object-environment contact sets and decoupled multi-contact dynamics. These are strong assumptions, especially during flipping, tipping, rolling, or simultaneous multi-contact interaction. Lemma 3 only gives a boundedness argument; it does not establish accuracy or useful dependence on contact geometry, active-set changes, force magnitude, or the off-diagonal blocks of the Delassus matrix.

The paper should state the regularity assumptions required for Lipschitz continuity of the LCP solution and provide empirical SCM error validation against a higher-fidelity contact solver.
\answer
Thank you for this important comment. We agree that SCM relies on strong assumptions and should include a more thorough analysis of modeling errors. Our motivation is that high-fidelity contact dynamics and complete contact detection are both nonsmooth and computationally expensive for optimization. Moreover, high-fidelity contact dynamics are highly geometry-dependent and involve nonsmooth contact transitions, which can introduce numerical instability during optimization. Therefore, the role of SCM is only to predict the correct object motion trend rather than provide high-fidelity contact simulation. In practical deployment, the influence of SCM approximation errors on SCSP is further alleviated by the high-frequency receding-horizon updates of the framework.

We need to correct our previous analysis. The error-bound lemma can become very loose when the Delassus matrix is rank deficient, because its smallest eigenvalue can be dominated by the artificial regularization term. In addition, a global Lipschitz-continuity statement for the LCP solution is difficult to justify when the contact set switches. We therefore removed the erroneous lemma and proof and replaced this theoretical claim with empirical contact-model evaluation.

We evaluate sliding, sticking, contact transition (including separation and new-contact establishment), and \ndel{a reference-path flipping rollout in MuJoCo. The ground truth uses LCP contact dynamics, while contact points are obtained from MuJoCo collision checking. All models use $h=0.002\,\mathrm{s}$. We compare MuJoCo's underlying QP model, ComFree, ContactSDF, and SCM, and report post-step linear- and angular-velocity errors as means, standard deviations, and P95 values in Table~\ref{tab:scm_velocity_error}.} \nadd{rotation and flipping rollouts. The references are the native contact solvers of MuJoCo and IsaacGym: at every step, each engine is reset to the common MuJoCo reference state and advanced once, and SCM reports the one-step prediction of Eq.~(27) at $h=0.002\,\mathrm{s}$ (diagonal Delassus estimate refined by $K=3$ unrolled Gauss--Seidel sweeps, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$, $T=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N\,s}$). The contact state of each sample is classified from the loaded contacts of the reference state (contact transition if any contact has a normal relative speed above $0.01\,\mathrm{m/s}$, otherwise sliding if any contact slips faster than $1\,\mathrm{mm/s}$, otherwise sticking), and the contact-mode samples are further grouped by the active-contact count $n_c\in\{1,2,\geq3\}$. The benchmark uses seven object meshes, 32 initial states per object and contact state, and 10 rollout windows of 250 steps per rollout state. Table~\ref{tab:scm_velocity_error_new} (Table~IV of the revised manuscript) reports the post-step linear- and angular-velocity errors (mean $\pm$ std), and the revised Fig.~11 further resolves the same errors per object geometry and contact state.}

\question{3}{The ranking strategy appears internally inconsistent}

The paper defines
\begin{equation}
 \rho=\frac{\ell_{\text{near}}-\hat{\ell}_{\text{min}}}{\max(\hat{\ell}_{\text{max}}-\hat{\ell}_{\text{min}},\epsilon)}.
\end{equation}
With this definition, smaller $\rho$ means that $p_{\text{near}}$ is closer to the best candidate, and larger $\rho$ means it is worse. However, Eq. (39) sets $\kappa_t=\mathbf{1}(\rho_t>\bar{\rho})$ and states that $\kappa_t=1$ means $p_{\text{near}}$ is sufficiently good. This reverses the intended meaning. Eq. (40) is also difficult to reconcile with Algorithm 2 and the described lifting/placing phases.

This section should be rewritten as a clear finite-state or hysteresis rule. As written, the trigger logic is not mathematically consistent.

\answer
Thank you for pointing out this inconsistency. We agree that the original definition of the ranking score was unclear and could lead to a reversed interpretation. In the revised manuscript, $\rho_t$ is explicitly defined as the normalized ranking score of $p_{\mathrm{near}}$ based on its cost reduction $\Delta \ell_t^{\mathrm{near}}$ and the range of cost reductions $[\Delta \ell_t^{\mathrm{min}},\Delta \ell_t^{\mathrm{max}}]$ among all candidate contact locations:
\begin{equation}\label{equ:ranking1}
\rho_t=
\Pi_{[0,1]}\!\left(
\frac{
\Delta\ell_t^{\mathrm{near}}
-\Delta\ell_t^{\mathrm{min}}
}{\max(
\Delta\ell_t^{\mathrm{max}}
-\Delta\ell_t^{\mathrm{min}}, \epsilon)
}
\right)
\end{equation}
It represents the relative ranking of the improvement achieved by $p_{\mathrm{near}}$ compared with other candidates. Therefore, a smaller $\rho_t$ indicates that $p_{\mathrm{near}}$ provides less improvement and has a worse ranking, while a larger $\rho_t$ indicates that $p_{\mathrm{near}}$ is more competitive.

We further clarify that the previous formulation using the absolute cost $\ell$ and the revised formulation using the cost reduction $\Delta\ell$ are equivalent under min-max normalization, except for the ordering convention. Specifically, since the cost reduction is defined as $\Delta \ell_t^i
=\ell(\boldsymbol{x}_t)-\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t^+)$, we can obtain that
\begin{equation}
\rho_t
=
\Pi_{[0,1]}\!\left(\frac{
\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{+,\mathrm{min}})
-
\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{+,\mathrm{near}})
}{
\max(
\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{+,\mathrm{min}})
-
\ell(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t}^{+,\mathrm{max}}),
\epsilon)
}\right). \label{equ:correct_derivation}
\end{equation}
The above derivation shows that the previous formulation using the absolute cost $\ell$ was equivalent in principle but introduced ambiguity due to the reversed ordering convention. To avoid this confusion, we have rewritten the Ranking Strategy subsection in Section IV-B and reformulated $\rho_t$ directly based on the cost reduction $\Delta\ell$. The revised manuscript also replaces the earlier counter-based switching rule with EMA smoothing of the binary trigger\oldadd{, $\bar{\kappa}_{t+1}=(1-\beta)\bar{\kappa}_t+\beta\kappa_t$, and thresholds the filtered trigger to obtain the binary phase variable $\gamma_{t+1}=\mathbf{1}(\bar{\kappa}_{t+1}\geq 1/2)\in\{0,1\}$ used by CPO (Eq.~(36)): $\gamma_t=0$ selects the lifting phase toward $\hat{p}_r^*$ and $\gamma_t=1$ selects the placing phase toward $p_{\mathrm{near}}$, so Eq.~(36), Algorithm~2, and the lifting/placing phases now share one explicit rule}. To remove the previous smoothing strategy's dependence on contact estimation---in particular, the $T_3$ counter that counted consecutive contact steps---we use an EMA that is independent of contact-state estimates.

\question{4}{The CPO potential costs appear incorrectly specified}

The lifting cost uses
\begin{equation}
 \ell_{\text{obs}}=\log(\lVert p_{\text{ee}}-p_o\rVert^2+\epsilon),
\end{equation}
inside a distance threshold and minimizes it. This is not a repulsive potential unless the weight is negative, which is not stated. The placing cost says that $\ell_{\text{con}}$ attracts the end-effector to the reference contact point $p_{\text{ref}}$, but the formula uses $p_o$ instead of $p_{\text{ref}}$:
\begin{equation}
 \ell_{\text{con}}=\log(\lVert p_{\text{ee}}-p_o\rVert^2+\epsilon).
\end{equation}
This should be corrected or explained. A natural formulation would explicitly include a term such as $\lVert p_{\text{ee}}-p_{\text{ref}}\rVert^2$.

\answer
Thank you for identifying these inconsistencies. We have revised Section IV-C (highlighted in red). Specifically, the sign of the object repulsive potential $\ell_{\mathrm{obs}}$ has been corrected to ensure a repulsive behavior, and the contact attraction term $\ell_{\mathrm{con}}$ has been modified to attract the end-effector toward an inward offset from the reference contact point $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ along its surface normal, with $\delta=0.02\,\mathrm{m}$. We also removed the previous attraction term toward the object geometric center $\boldsymbol{p}_o$. That term was intended to maintain sufficient contact under contact-estimation errors, but it could oppose the attraction toward $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and create a force balance that traps the optimizer in a local minimum. The revised placing cost is:
\begingroup
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\mathrm{con}}=\left\|\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{ee}}-\left(\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}-\delta\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{ref}}\right)\right\|^2.
\label{eq:placing_cost_reply}
\end{equation}
\endgroup
This removes the conflicting center-attraction term and yielded good empirical performance in the revised experiments.

\question{5}{The result analysis does not isolate the proposed contributions}

The experiments mainly show that the full SCSP system outperforms several baselines in aggregate success rate. They do not sufficiently explain which proposed mechanism causes which improvement. The causal link between the stated contributions and the results is therefore incomplete.

In particular, the paper should report evidence for each component:
\begin{itemize}
 \item CSO: quality of selected contact priors, contact-location maps, correlation between CSO cost and execution success, and comparison against random, nearest, heuristic, and multi-start contact selection.
 \item SCM: prediction error against a higher-fidelity contact solver, separated by contact count, contact mode, object geometry, and flipping/rotation phase.
 \item Ranking strategy: switch frequency, time spent tracking $\hat{p}_r^*$ versus $p_{\text{near}}$, failure cases caused by switching, and sensitivity to $\bar{\rho},T_1,T_2,T_3$.
 \item CPO objective: ablation of lift, place, object-center attraction, and $p_{\text{ref}}$ tracking terms.
 \item Robustness: failure categories separating perception error, contact estimation error, slippage, control saturation, and poor prior selection.
\end{itemize}
Without these analyses, claims about robustness, diversity, and the specific benefit of each module remain qualitative.

\answer
For CSO, we directly apply the optimized contact wrench at the selected contact and roll out the manipulation. The direct-force results in Table~V achieve nearly 100\% ideal success for SCM, which supports the quality of the selected contact priors. Figure~9 visualizes the contact maps for the elephant and bunny flipping tasks, including the CSO-optimal contact $p_r^*$, the nearest contact $p_{\mathrm{near}}$, and the physical contact observed during execution. In Section~V-B, ``Functional Analysis of SCSP Components,'' A1 follows the optimal CSO contact, A2 always uses the nearest contact, and A3 uses a coarse heuristic contact model; these results isolate optimal, nearest, and heuristic selection. We did not add separate random and multi-start baselines: CSO already evaluates the candidate set in parallel, which provides the multi-start computation pattern, while purely random contacts are too unreliable to yield a meaningful executable comparison.

For SCM, Table~IV reports linear- and angular-velocity errors \ndel{and P95 values} for contact transition, sliding, sticking, and the \ndel{reference-path flipping rollout} \nadd{rotation and flipping rollouts, with the contact-mode samples separated by the active-contact count $n_c\in\{1,2,\geq3\}$; Fig.~11 further resolves the errors per object geometry and contact state}. The benchmark uses seven object meshes with varied irregular geometries used in the experiments\oldadd{, 32 initial states per object and contact state,} and \ndel{an LCP reference with MJWarp collision detection} \nadd{the native contact solvers of MuJoCo and IsaacGym as references}. The direct-force rollout results in Table~V further test whether the predicted contact wrenches are executable; the high ideal success rates across models show that the trajectories are generally feasible, while the deployed results show that higher model fidelity alone does not guarantee better task performance.

For the ranking strategy, Table~VI reports the CSO-optimal-point switching frequency and physical contact-switching frequency. Table~II compares execution time for SCSP with A1 and A2, showing the efficiency difference between ranking-based execution, strict optimal-contact tracking, and nearest-contact execution. Table~VI also reports the normalized advantage of an individual ranking decision, computed from a time-discounted success reward, thereby measuring its effect on execution efficiency and outcome. Table~VIII reports failure categories and recovery rates for real hardware, including slippage \oldadd{and control saturation. Contact switching itself does not directly cause failure, so it is not counted as a failure category; instead, for the events that can topple the object after a contact switch, Table~VIII reports the number of occurrences and the recovery rate, i.e., the number of trials in which the robot successfully re-adjusted after the object fell over and still completed the task}. Finally, Fig.~10 evaluates sensitivity to the acceptance threshold $\bar{\rho}$ and EMA coefficient $\beta$. The earlier $T_1,T_2,T_3$ counter-based smoothing is replaced by contact-estimation-independent EMA smoothing.

For CPO, A6 removes the lift-and-place cost and directly solves an obstacle-avoidance trajectory-optimization problem, while A7 \oldadd{solves the same CPO problem without contact guidance using MPPI (see our reply to Q6)}. In the current formulation, the contact target is an inward-offset (penetrating) point $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}-\delta\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathrm{ref}}$, rather than an attraction term toward the object center. In the real-world ablation A9, we replace this inward-offset target with the estimated surface point $\boldsymbol{p}_{\mathrm{ref}}$; this surface-attraction design often prevents the robot from descending to establish contact. Table~VIII reports the resulting contact-estimation-related errors together with the success and recovery outcomes. The same table counts perception error, contact-estimation error, slippage, control saturation, and poor prior selection, and reports the recovery rate after each event. An event does not necessarily imply final failure because SCSP replanning can sometimes correct it; the recovery rate therefore counts the trials that still succeed after the event. Together with the CSO, SCM, ranking, and hardware analyses above, these experiments quantify the proposed components within the tested tasks.

\question{6}{Baseline coverage and comparison protocols need strengthening}

The Sampling comparison is not fully controlled because the paper replaces the original contact model with $f_{cf}$. This may create a new baseline rather than a direct implementation of the cited method. The DyWA comparison also needs careful framing: DyWA is described in Table I as lacking generalization, although its stated purpose is generalizable nonprehensile manipulation. The paper should define exactly what ``generalization'' means.

The baseline set is also incomplete relative to the scope of the claims. The following comparisons or discussions are particularly relevant:

\question{3}{Significance}

- The core algorithmic simplification presented in the method, namely
diagonalization of the Delassus matrix in the SCM for smooth, efficient
optimization in CSO, is heavily inspired, as noted by the authors, by
Jin’s complementarity-free MPC work [27], which somewhat similarly
diagonalizes the Delassus matrix as a scaled identity matrix. Where Jin
justifies this extreme physical simplification with receding-horizon
control, the authors propose a heuristic-driven smoothing over this
simplified, open-loop contact selection optimization via the ranking
strategy. Both make fairly strong assumptions about rigid bodies and
validate the approach empirically.

- The authors provide finite bounds on the error of this simplification
in Lemma 3. However, if I am understanding correctly, the
defined bounds are very large: $\sigma_1/\lambda_{\text{min}}$ (Eqn. 58),
where $\lambda_{\text{min}}$ is the minimal eigenvalue of the Delassus matrix
($W_{\text{env}}$), which under rank-deficient Jacobians (essentially the
default case) is determined by the selected, typically very small
regularizing term R in Eq. 22, which can be increased but is itself
already a physically spurious quantity. I also would appreciate some
extrapolation on ``standard perturbation theory for LCP." Generally, I
think the authors introduce limited theoretical novelty, but that
should not detract from the work’s strong and broad results, including
real-world deployment and practicality (not needing mocap).

\answer

Thank you for the careful assessment. We are indeed deeply inspired by Jin's complementarity-free simplification: we construct a closed-form contact model so that CSO can evaluate contact candidates efficiently. 

We also need to correct an omission in the previous manuscript. The \ndel{one-step global projection of the environment impulse} \nadd{$K=3$ unrolled Gauss--Seidel sweeps over the environment contacts (Eq.~(27))} \ndel{was} \nadd{were} not described. Directly constructing $D$ from the diagonal entries of the Delassus matrix is an aggressive simplification and can introduce substantial model bias, which makes the corresponding error bound loose; the \ndel{global projection} \nadd{sweeps, in which each contact force is evaluated from the object velocity already updated by the preceding contacts and sweeps,} \ndel{corrects} \nadd{correct} part of this bias. Nevertheless, as the reviewer observes, the bound can still become very large under rank-deficient Jacobians. SCM can represent separation of retained old contacts, but newly created contacts are absent from the current contact set and inevitably introduce additional prediction bias.

For the same reason, the previous lemma and proof are not useful as general guarantees. The earlier appeal to standard perturbation theory for LCPs was also inappropriate: perturbations near a contact-set boundary can cause large changes, and SCM does not smooth those contact-set transitions. We have therefore removed the error-bound lemma and its proof and no longer present this perturbation argument as a theoretical guarantee.

We instead validate SCM empirically as a feasible contact model for contact selection. The velocity-error table in the manuscript \ndel{compares MuJoCo's high-fidelity QP/LCP contact simulation, ComFree, ContactSDF, and SCM} \nadd{(Table~IV) compares the one-step SCM prediction with the native contact solvers of MuJoCo and IsaacGym} over contact transition, sliding, sticking, and \ndel{a reference-path flipping rollout in MuJoCo} \nadd{rotation and flipping rollouts, with the contact-mode samples grouped by the active-contact count $n_c\in\{1,2,\geq3\}$}, reporting linear- and angular-velocity prediction errors\nadd{; Fig.~11 resolves the same errors per object geometry}. SCM remains within an acceptable range across these contact states. We then generate optimal contact-wrench trajectories with each model and test their feasibility in the direct-force evaluation. The contact-guidance results \nadd{(Table~V)} show that all models produce largely feasible trajectories, while \ndel{the closed-form models can perform better in deployment than the higher-fidelity model} \nadd{SCM performs better in deployment than the native engine models (0.85 versus 0.46 for M1 in MuJoCo and 0.88 versus 0.76 for M2 in IsaacGym)}. This demonstrates that model fidelity is not directly proportional to task success and motivates using SCM for CSO.
